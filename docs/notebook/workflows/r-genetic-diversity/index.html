PART I

Goal

Everything so far has described a single population at a single locus. Real species are collections of populations — forest stands scattered across a landscape — and their genetic variation can be divided into two parts:

  • Within populations: how genetically variable is each stand?
  • Among populations: how different are the stands from each other?

This module introduces the standard way population genetics quantifies that split: within-population gene diversity (\(H_S\)), total gene diversity (\(H_T\)), and the fixation index

\[F_{ST} = \frac{H_T - H_S}{H_T},\]

the fraction of a species’ total genetic diversity that sits among populations rather than within them.

In the hands-on part we simulate multiple populations with different allele frequencies, compute \(H_S\), \(H_T\), and \(F_{ST}\), build a pairwise \(F_{ST}\) matrix, and finish by adding geography — testing whether populations that are farther apart are also more genetically different (isolation by distance).

Learning objectives

  • Explain the difference between genetic diversity within populations and genetic differentiation among populations.
  • Define and compute \(H_S\), \(H_T\), and \(F_{ST}\).
  • Interpret \(F_{ST}\) values and know their rough benchmarks.
  • Explain how genetic drift and gene flow push \(F_{ST}\) in opposite directions.
  • Describe isolation by distance as a hypothesis about how gene flow decays with distance.
  • Simulate multiple populations, compute a pairwise \(F_{ST}\) matrix, and visualize it as a heatmap.
  • Test the relationship between genetic and geographic distance.

Two levels of genetic variation

Imagine sampling red spruce trees from six stands across the Appalachians. At any SNP, some of the variation you see exists inside each stand — individuals within a stand carry different genotypes. But the stands themselves also differ: an allele that is common in a northern stand may be rare in a southern one.

These two levels answer different biological questions:

  • Within-population diversity matters for a stand’s evolutionary potential — can it adapt to future conditions if it retains variation?
  • Among-population differentiation matters for conservation and for local adaptation — are stands locally distinct, and would moving seed between them swamp or rescue local adaptation?

A species can have high diversity and low differentiation (most variation within stands), or low diversity and high differentiation (stands small and genetically distinct). These are not the same quantity, and \(F_{ST}\) exists precisely to separate them.

The evolutionary forces

TipConcept review: genetic drift

Drift is the change in allele frequency caused by random sampling of gametes between generations. It is strongest in small populations, and it pushes isolated populations apart: each stand’s allele frequency random-walks independently, so stands that started identical gradually diverge. Drift also erodes variation within populations, eventually fixing or losing alleles.

Read more: Genetic drift (LibreTexts)

TipConcept review: gene flow

Gene flow is the movement of alleles between populations — by migrating individuals, or for trees, by seed and especially pollen dispersal. Gene flow pushes populations together: migrants carry alleles from one stand into another, making allele frequencies more similar and countering the divergence drift creates.

Read more: Gene flow and mutation (LibreTexts)

Drift and gene flow are opponents. Drift makes populations diverge at a rate that scales with \(1/N_e\); gene flow homogenizes them at a rate that scales with the migration rate \(m\). For the classic island model, their balance produces an equilibrium

\[F_{ST} \approx \frac{1}{4N_em + 1},\]

where \(N_em\) is the effective number of migrants per generation. A widely used rule of thumb follows from this formula: even one migrant per generation (\(N_em \approx 1\)) is enough to keep populations from diverging strongly by drift alone. This is why \(F_{ST}\) is often interpreted informally as a measure of gene flow — high \(F_{ST}\) implies little of it.

WarningFST is about neutral structure

The \(F_{ST}\)–gene-flow relationship holds for neutral loci. Low \(F_{ST}\) at neutral markers does not mean populations are interchangeable: locally adapted populations can be nearly identical at neutral SNPs while differing strongly at loci under selection. That distinction — neutral structure versus adaptive divergence — is exactly the gap that genotype–environment association methods address.

Partitioning diversity: \(H_S\), \(H_T\), and \(F_{ST}\)

Take a set of populations, each with its own allele frequency \(p_i\) for allele A. Two heterozygosities summarize the whole system:

  • Within-population gene diversity, \(H_S\): the average expected heterozygosity of the populations, \[H_S = \overline{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}.\] It answers: if you drew two alleles at random from the same population, how often would they differ?

  • Total gene diversity, \(H_T\): the expected heterozygosity computed from the allele frequency of the pooled sample (all populations thrown together), \[H_T = 2\, \bar{p}\, (1 - \bar{p}), \quad \bar{p} = \text{mean allele frequency}.\] It answers: if you drew two alleles at random from the whole species, how often would they differ?

If every population had the same allele frequency, then \(H_S = H_T\): pooling adds no information, and there is no differentiation. The more the populations’ allele frequencies differ, the more \(H_T\) exceeds \(H_S\) — because in the pooled sample, alleles drawn from different populations often differ. \(F_{ST}\) is simply the share of the total that this between-population component represents:

\[F_{ST} = \frac{H_T - H_S}{H_T}.\]

\(F_{ST}\) ranges from 0 (populations identical) toward 1 (populations fixed for different alleles). The subscript letters are historical: \(S\) = subpopulation (within), \(T\) = total (the whole set), and \(F\) comes from “fixation”.

Worked example: \(F_{ST}\) by hand

Two populations, one biallelic SNP, allele-A frequencies \(p_1 = 0.80\) and \(p_2 = 0.20\):

  1. Within: \(H_1 = 2(0.80)(0.20) = 0.32\) and \(H_2 = 2(0.20)(0.80) = 0.32\), so \(H_S = 0.32\).
  2. Total: \(\bar{p} = (0.80 + 0.20)/2 = 0.50\), so \(H_T = 2(0.50)(0.50) = 0.50\).
  3. Differentiation: \[F_{ST} = \frac{0.50 - 0.32}{0.50} = 0.36.\]

Interpretation: 36% of the total genetic diversity at this locus lies among populations; only 64% sits within them. Note also the subtle point this example hides: each population still has plenty of heterozygosity (\(H_S = 0.32\)) — differentiation does not require the populations themselves to be invariant.

How big is big? Interpreting \(F_{ST}\)

Wright’s rough guidelines, still widely quoted:

\(F_{ST}\) Interpretation
0 – 0.05 little differentiation
0.05 – 0.15 moderate differentiation
0.15 – 0.25 great differentiation
> 0.25 very great differentiation

Treat these as landmarks, not thresholds. \(F_{ST}\) depends on the marker type (microsatellites behave differently from SNPs), on diversity itself (\(F_{ST}\) is bounded above when the minor allele is rare), and on the scale of sampling. In wind-pollinated forest trees, neutral \(F_{ST}\) typically lands between 0.02 and 0.10 — long-distance pollen flow keeps stands surprisingly similar — so a value of 0.15 would already be notable for a conifer.

Isolation by distance

If dispersal is limited, nearby populations exchange more migrants than distant ones. Sewall Wright (1943) called the resulting pattern isolation by distance (IBD): genetic differentiation should increase with geographic distance, because gene flow — the homogenizing force — decays as distance grows.

IBD turns the question into a testable relationship:

  1. Sample populations across a landscape and record their positions.
  2. Compute two distance matrices: geographic distance between every pair of sites, and genetic distance (here, pairwise \(F_{ST}\)).
  3. Ask whether the two matrices are correlated. Farther pairs should be more differentiated.

The standard test is the Mantel test, which correlates two distance matrices and compares the observed correlation against permutations that shuffle population labels. We will use vegan::mantel(), from the package installed in an earlier module. A significant positive relationship between genetic and geographic distance is evidence that dispersal limitation — not just drift or history — structures the species’ genetic variation.

NoteClassic references
  • Wright, S. (1943). Isolation by distance. Genetics 28: 114–138.
  • Rousset, F. (1997). Genetic differentiation and estimation of gene flow from F-statistics under isolation by distance. Genetics 145: 1219–1224 — proposes regressing \(F_{ST}/(1 - F_{ST})\) on distance for biallelic markers.

PART II

Hands-on: multiple populations in R

We reuse sim_popgen() from the previous module to create six populations with a gradient of allele frequencies — a cline, as if allele A were common in the south and rare in the north:

Importantsim_popgen() : custom function 
# Simulate one population at one biallelic locus, then summarize it
#   n : number of individuals
#   p : frequency of allele A (0 to 1)
#
# Genotypes are coded 0/1/2:
#   0 = AA, 1 = Aa, 2 = aa
sim_popgen <- function(n, p) {


  q <- 1 - p                       # frequency of allele a


  # 1. Simulate genotypes -------------------------------------------
  #    Under Hardy-Weinberg:  AA = p^2,  Aa = 2pq,  aa = q^2
  genotype <- sample(0:2,
                     size = n,
                     replace = TRUE,
                     prob = c(p^2, 2 * p * q, q^2))


  # 2. Genotype frequencies ----------------------------------------
  geno_freq <- table(genotype) / n


  # 3. Allele frequencies --------------------------------------------
  #    Each genotype value IS an allele-a count, so sum / (2 * n)
  f_a <- sum(genotype) / (2 * n)
  f_A <- 1 - f_a


  # 4. Heterozygosities ---------------------------------------------
  Ho <- sum(genotype == 1) / n      # observed: fraction of Aa
  He <- 2 * f_A * f_a              # expected under H-W: 2pq


  # 5. Print everything -----------------------------------------------
  cat("Allele frequencies:\n")
  cat("  p(A) =", round(f_A, 3), " q(a) =", round(f_a, 3), "\n\n")


  cat("Genotype frequencies:\n")
  print(round(geno_freq, 3))


  cat("\nHeterozygosity:\n")
  cat("  Ho =", round(Ho, 3), " (observed)\n")
  cat("  He =", round(He, 3), " (expected, H-W)\n")


  # Return the results in a list so you can reuse them
  list(genotype = genotype,
       allele_freq = c(A = f_A, a = f_a),
       Ho = Ho,
       He = He)
}

Two R details worth pausing on:

  • A list (pops[[i]] <- ...) is the natural container for a set of vectors that all belong together. Double brackets [[ ]] assign to one slot of the list.
  • sapply(pops, function(g) ...) applies the little function to each element of the list and simplifies the results into one named vector — it replaces a for loop when you only want to compute a value per element.

Because genotypes are coded 0/1/2 (copies of allele a), the frequency of allele A is 1 - sum(g) / (2 * length(g)) — the divide-by-2 rule from Part I, inverted.

Heterozygosity within populations: \(H_S\)

Each population’s expected heterozygosity is \(2p(1-p)\) at its own observed allele frequency. \(H_S\) is their average:

# Expected heterozygosity WITHIN each population
He_pops <- sapply(pops, function(g) {
  p <- 1 - sum(g) / (2 * length(g))   # frequency of allele A
  2 * p * (1 - p)
})

Hs <- mean(He_pops)
round(c(He_pops = He_pops, Hs = Hs), 3)
He_pops.Pop_A He_pops.Pop_B He_pops.Pop_C He_pops.Pop_D He_pops.Pop_E 
        0.124         0.339         0.444         0.406         0.180 
He_pops.Pop_F            Hs 
        0.124         0.270

Notice something interesting in the output: the populations at the ends of the cline (\(p \approx 0.05\) and \(p \approx 0.95\)) have the lowest heterozygosity, and the middle populations the highest. Heterozygosity is maximized at \(p = 0.5\) — a population can be highly differentiated and still have little internal variation when its allele is near fixation.

Total gene diversity and global \(F_{ST}\): \(H_T\)

\(H_T\) uses the allele frequency of the pooled sample, as if we ignored population labels entirely:

# Pool all individuals, ignoring population labels
all_g <- unlist(pops)
p_tot <- 1 - sum(all_g) / (2 * length(all_g))
HT <- 2 * p_tot * (1 - p_tot)

FST <- (HT - Hs) / HT
round(c(p_tot = p_tot, Hs = Hs, HT = HT, FST = FST), 3)
p_tot    Hs    HT   FST 
0.528 0.270 0.498 0.459

unlist() flattens the list of genotype vectors into one long vector — the genetic equivalent of pouring all six stands into one bag. From here, \(F_{ST}\) is one line: the among-population share of the total.

Pairwise \(F_{ST}\)

The global \(F_{ST}\) collapses all six populations into one number. Often more informative is the pairwise matrix: \(F_{ST}\) for every pair of populations, showing which stands are similar and which differ. The logic is identical to the global calculation, applied to just two populations at a time:

# FST for ONE pair of populations (same recipe as the global version)
fst_pair <- function(gi, gj) {
  p_i <- 1 - sum(gi) / (2 * length(gi))   # allele A frequency, pop i
  p_j <- 1 - sum(gj) / (2 * length(gj))   # allele A frequency, pop j

  H_i <- 2 * p_i * (1 - p_i)              # heterozygosity within i
  H_j <- 2 * p_j * (1 - p_j)              # heterozygosity within j
  H_s <- (H_i + H_j) / 2                  # average within-pop diversity

  p_t <- (p_i + p_j) / 2                  # pooled allele frequency
  H_t <- 2 * p_t * (1 - p_t)              # total diversity

  (H_t - H_s) / H_t                       # FST for this pair
}

# Loop over every pair and fill a k x k matrix
k <- length(pops)
fst_mat <- matrix(NA, k, k,
                  dimnames = list(pop_names, pop_names))

for (i in 1:k) {
  for (j in 1:k) {
    fst_mat[i, j] <- fst_pair(pops[[i]], pops[[j]])
  }
}
diag(fst_mat) <- 0          # a population compared with itself

In heat.colors(), red means low \(F_{ST}\) (similar populations) and pale yellow means high. The matrix is symmetric, so the map is a mirror image across its diagonal; a perfect cline shows a smooth gradient from the Pop_A corner to the Pop_F corner. image() also flips the first matrix index onto the y-axis — a classic R gotcha, and harmless here only because the matrix is symmetric.

TipA note on negative FST

With real (or simulated) sampling noise, a pairwise \(F_{ST}\) can come out slightly negative — sampling made the two populations look more different within than between. A negative value means “differentiation indistinguishable from zero”; it is not an error in the formula.

How \(F_{ST}\) changes as populations diverge

The cline above is one snapshot. To see the whole range of behavior, sweep across scenarios — from identical populations to maximally divergent ones — and compute the global \(F_{ST}\) of each:

# Global FST for a set of populations defined by their allele frequencies
global_fst <- function(p_vec, n = 30) {

  # simulate each population's genotypes directly with sample()
  # (avoids sim_popgen()'s printed output)
  pops <- lapply(p_vec, function(p) {
    sample(0:2, size = n, replace = TRUE,
           prob = c(p^2, 2 * p * (1 - p), (1 - p)^2))
  })

  # Hs: average within-population heterozygosity
  Hs <- mean(sapply(pops, function(g) {
    p <- 1 - sum(g) / (2 * length(g))
    2 * p * (1 - p)
  }))

  # HT: heterozygosity of the pooled sample
  all_g <- unlist(pops)
  p_tot <- 1 - sum(all_g) / (2 * length(all_g))
  HT <- 2 * p_tot * (1 - p_tot)

  (HT - Hs) / HT
}

# Three scenarios: similar, moderate, divergent
scenarios <- list(
  similar   = rep(0.50, 6),
  moderate  = seq(0.35, 0.65, length.out = 6),
  divergent = seq(0.05, 0.95, length.out = 6)
)

fst_values <- sapply(scenarios, global_fst)

barplot(fst_values, col = "#583C88",
        ylab = "Global FST", ylim = c(0, 1),
        main = "FST rises as populations diverge")

What you should see:

  • Similar populations (identical input \(p\)) give \(F_{ST} \approx 0\) — not exactly zero, because finite samples (\(n = 30\)) wobble, but close.
  • Divergent populations push \(F_{ST}\) high; with allele frequencies spread from 0.05 to 0.95, most of the total diversity is among populations.
  • The relationship is not linear in the spread of allele frequencies — the same spread matters more when it crosses 0.5, and \(F_{ST}\) is capped well below 1 unless alleles are near fixation in each population.

Adding geography: isolation by distance

Finally, give the populations locations. Our cline becomes a transect: six stands sampled at regular intervals along a line, with allele A increasing from one end to the other. Because allele frequencies were assigned in geographic order, nearby populations are genetically similar and distant ones differ — isolation by distance, by construction.

# Sampling locations: six stands along a transect
sites <- data.frame(
  pop = pop_names,
  lon = c(-87.0, -86.8, -86.6, -86.4, -86.2, -86.0),
  lat = rep(40.4, 6)
)

# Geographic distance between every pair of sites
geo_mat <- as.matrix(dist(cbind(sites$lon, sites$lat)))
rownames(geo_mat) <- colnames(geo_mat) <- pop_names

# Genetic distance = pairwise FST (computed above)
plot(as.vector(geo_mat), as.vector(fst_mat),
     xlab = "Geographic distance (degrees)",
     ylab = "Pairwise FST",
     main = "Isolation by distance")
abline(lm(as.vector(fst_mat) ~ as.vector(geo_mat)),
       col = "red", lwd = 2)
cor(as.vector(geo_mat), as.vector(fst_mat))
[1] 0.9459717

The points should trend upward: the farther apart two stands are, the more differentiated they are. The red line is a plain linear regression — good for seeing the trend, but it ignores that the points are not independent (each population appears in five pairs). The proper test is the Mantel test, which permutes population labels and asks whether the observed matrix correlation is stronger than chance:

A significant IBD signal tells you dispersal limitation contributes to structure; it does not rule out history or selection acting alongside it.

Exercise 1

# Add migrants drawn from the global allele frequency
add_migration <- function(g, m, p_tot) {
  n_mig <- round(m * length(g))            # how many migrants
  migrants <- sample(0:2, n_mig, replace = TRUE,
                     prob = c(p_tot^2, 2 * p_tot * (1 - p_tot),
                              (1 - p_tot)^2))
  c(sample(g, length(g) - n_mig), migrants)  # residents + migrants
}

m_values <- c(0, 0.05, 0.25)
sapply(m_values, function(m) {
  mig_pops <- lapply(pops, add_migration, m = m, p_tot = p_tot)
  # same Hs / HT / FST recipe as global_fst(), on mig_pops
  Hs <- mean(sapply(mig_pops, function(g) {
    p <- 1 - sum(g) / (2 * length(g)); 2 * p * (1 - p)
  }))
  all_g <- unlist(mig_pops)
  p_t <- 1 - sum(all_g) / (2 * length(all_g))
  (2 * p_t * (1 - p_t) - Hs) / (2 * p_t * (1 - p_t))
})

What you should see: \(F_{ST}\) falls as \(m\) grows. Even 5% migration pulls the populations noticeably toward the pooled frequency — the one-migrant-per-generation rule in action. At \(m = 0.25\), the cline has largely dissolved.

Exercise 3

Wrap the divergent scenario in a per-locus loop with random clines:

locus_fst <- replicate(20, {
  p_cline <- sort(runif(6, 0.05, 0.95))    # a random cline for this locus
  global_fst(p_cline)
})
c(single_locus_example = locus_fst[1],
  mean_over_20_loci    = mean(locus_fst))
single_locus_example    mean_over_20_loci 
           0.2131148            0.2513456

What you should see: any single locus can give an erratic \(F_{ST}\) (clines vary, sampling noise adds more), but the average over 20 loci is much more stable. This is why empirical studies never quote a one-locus \(F_{ST}\) — the genome-wide average is the estimate.

plot(as.vector(geo_mat), as.vector(fst_mat) / (1 - as.vector(fst_mat)),
     xlab = "Geographic distance (degrees)",
     ylab = "FST / (1 - FST)",
     main = "IBD with Rousset's transform")
abline(lm(I(as.vector(fst_mat) / (1 - as.vector(fst_mat))) ~
            as.vector(geo_mat)), col = "red", lwd = 2)

What you should see: the transform stretches small \(F_{ST}\) values apart near zero, roughly linearizing the relationship — which is exactly why Rousset proposed it. With our strong artificial cline the improvement may be modest; with real, weakly structured data it is often substantial.
